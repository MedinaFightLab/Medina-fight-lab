Strength & Power

Upper Body Ballistics & Plyometrics for Striking: A Practical Guide

Dylan Medina | Medina Fight Lab

A punch lasts a fraction of a second. In that window you can't produce your maximum strength — you can only produce as much force as time allows. That's why striking power is less about how much you can bench and more about how fast you can express force. Ballistic and plyometric training targets exactly that: the rate of force development (RFD) and the stretch-shortening cycle (SSC) that make a punch snap instead of push.

Ballistic vs. Plyometric: What's the Difference?

Ballistic exercises are accelerated through the entire range and released or left unrestrained — a medicine ball throw, a bench press throw, a landmine punch. There's no deceleration phase, so you keep accelerating to the end, just like a punch. Plyometric exercises use a fast stretch-then-shorten action to exploit the SSC — plyo push-ups, drop push-ups, and rebound ball catches. Both train the nervous system to recruit high-threshold motor units quickly, and both transfer best when the movement resembles the skill.

What the Evidence Says

Research on upper-body plyometric and medicine ball training in athletes and combat sport populations generally shows improvements in throwing velocity, upper-body power, and in some studies punch force or speed, particularly when added on top of a strength base. The caveat: gains in punch performance are smaller and less consistent than gains in general power tests, because punching is a whole-body, skill-dependent action. Power work supports your technique; it doesn't replace it. And the effect is larger for athletes with less strength training history, so building a strength foundation first still matters.

The Punch Is a Leg-to-Hand Chain

Remember that force in a cross or hook starts from the ground, travels through the hips and trunk, and finishes through the arm. The best upper-body ballistics are therefore whole-body throws, not isolated arm movements. For the rotational side of this chain, see our guide to rotational power for fighters. Here we focus on the pressing, pushing, and throwing patterns that load the shoulder girdle and arms.

The Core Exercises

1. Medicine Ball Throws

  • Chest pass / explosive push throw: a straight-line analog to the jab and cross. Use a 2–4 kg ball, wall or partner.
  • Rotational scoop or shot-put throw: drive from the back leg and rotate through the hips to mimic hooks and crosses.
  • Overhead slam: develops trunk-to-arm force transfer and is easy on the shoulder.

Keep the ball light enough that you move it fast. A ball so heavy that it slows the throw defeats the purpose.

2. Plyometric Push-Ups

  • Clap or hands-off push-ups: a simple entry to SSC loading of the pressing muscles.
  • Drop push-ups (hands on low plates or boxes): a more advanced option with a higher eccentric load — build up to them.
  • Band-assisted or incline variations: to scale the load for beginners.

3. Ballistic Pressing

  • Bench press throws (Smith machine or cage with safeties): load roughly 30–50% of 1RM and throw the bar, catching it safely. This removes the deceleration phase of a normal press.
  • Landmine press and landmine punch: shoulder-friendly, angled pressing that blends nicely with real punching mechanics.

How to Program It

Power work is high-quality, low-fatigue work. Treat it that way.

  • Volume: 3–5 sets of 3–5 reps per exercise, with 1–3 minutes of full rest. If speed drops, stop the set.
  • Placement: do it early in the session, after the warm-up and before heavy lifting or conditioning. Fatigue ruins the quality you're trying to train.
  • Frequency: 1–2 sessions per week is enough for most strikers. Keep total contacts low — roughly 20–40 explosive reps per session.
  • Pairing: a contrast set — a heavy press followed by a ballistic throw — is a time-efficient way to combine strength and speed.
  • In-camp: reduce volume but keep intensity. Two short sets of fast throws can maintain power with minimal fatigue before sparring.

Protect the Shoulders

Ballistic and plyometric loading is demanding on the shoulder, elbow, and wrist, and fighters already stress them heavily with bagwork and sparring. Build up gradually, avoid plyo push-ups if you have unresolved shoulder pain, and make sure you have a base of rotator cuff and scapular strength first. Our shoulder protocols cover that groundwork. Stop a set if technique breaks down, and don't add explosive work to a shoulder that's already flared up from a heavy sparring week.

Common Mistakes

  • Going too heavy: slow reps train strength, not speed.
  • Too much volume: power drills done to fatigue become conditioning, not power work.
  • Arms only: if the legs and hips aren't involved, it won't carry over to punching.
  • Skipping the strength base: ballistics work best on top of solid pressing, pulling, and trunk strength.

The Bottom Line

Punching power is about how quickly you can express force through a coordinated chain. Medicine ball throws, plyo push-ups, and ballistic presses train that quality — when they're light enough to move fast, low in volume, placed early in the session, and built on a strength and shoulder-health foundation. Keep them short, keep them fast, and keep practicing the actual punch. That combination builds a hand that arrives sooner and lands harder.
